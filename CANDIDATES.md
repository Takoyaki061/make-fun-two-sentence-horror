# Two-Sentence Horror — candidates and pick

Judge brief (from the kit): exactly two sentences, ≤400 chars. The judge
weighs, in order: (1) genuinely frightening; (2) the second sentence turns or
reveals something that makes the first sentence worse on a second read;
(3) horror implied rather than explained; (4) precise, concrete prose;
(5) original, not a familiar twist. Restraint over shock; gore doesn't count.

## Candidates

### A. The closet (rejected — familiar trope)
> Every night at 3 a.m., my daughter stands at the foot of my bed and whispers that the man in the closet is lonely. Tonight she didn't come, and the closet door is open.
- "Man in the closet" is a well-worn image; the twist is guessable.

### B. The lighthouse log
> The lighthouse log says the lamp went dark for six minutes on Tuesday, the only gap in forty years. The three ships that passed in those six minutes are still listed as missing.
- Strong reframe (a maintenance gap becomes the horror), fully implied, original setting. Weakness: the horror happens to strangers at a distance — less personal dread than the ward stories.

### C. The smart speaker (rejected — weak second sentence)
> My smart speaker answered a question I hadn't asked yet, in my own voice, before I opened my mouth. I unplugged it, but it kept talking from inside the wall.
- The second sentence explains rather than reframes; "device that won't die" is familiar.

### D. Flat 4B (rejected — familiar trope)
> The new tenant in 4B waves at me every morning from the window, and I've started waving back. The building manager told me today that 4B has been empty since the fire.
- Concrete, but the "empty flat with a waving tenant" shape is a known ghost-story outline.

### E. The drawing (rejected — familiar twist)
> My son's drawing of our family has five people in it, and there are four of us. The fifth one is standing behind me, and it has my face.
- "It has my face" is a known twist; the drawing device is overused.

### F. The photo booth
> The photo booth printed four pictures, but I only took three. In the fourth, I am asleep in my own bed, and someone is holding the camera.
- Very strong reframe: a fun public machine captured something private and violating. Concrete, implied, frightening. Weakness vs the pick: the second sentence is more a reveal than a re-reading of the first — the booth itself doesn't become worse, the bedroom does.

### G. Bed four — PICKED
> The night nurse leaves a glass of water by bed four, even though bed four has been empty for a month. Last night the glass was empty, and the sheets were warm.
- 158 characters, exactly two sentences.
- Criterion 1: "the sheets were warm" is a gut-punch — someone/something lay there minutes ago, in a bed that has been empty for a month.
- Criterion 2: the second sentence reframes the first completely. The nurse's kind routine stops being routine: the water was being drunk all along. On re-read, "leaves a glass of water" is no longer care, it's evidence.
- Criterion 3: nothing is explained. Who drank? What lay down? The reader fills in the worst.
- Criterion 4: every word earns its place — "night nurse", "bed four", "empty for a month", "warm". No adjectives wasted.
- Criterion 5: a hospital-ward haunting with a water glass is not one of the standard two-sentence-horror outlines (no mirror, no "it was me", no dead spouse, no phone call).

### H. The attic light
> The attic light turns itself on every night at midnight, though the switch has been broken for years. Yesterday I found a chair beneath the bulb, facing the stairs, still warm.
- Good and concrete, but "warm chair" and "attic light" are both familiar horror furniture; G's water glass is fresher and the ward setting is more original.

## Pick rationale

G beats F on criterion 2 (the first sentence itself becomes sinister on re-read,
not just the situation) and beats B on criterion 1 (the dread is immediate and
bodily — warm sheets — rather than abstract and distant). G beats H on
criterion 5 (fresher image). G is the strongest across the judge's ordered
criteria.
